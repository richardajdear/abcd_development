## 1. Scientific question and the logic of the design

The goal is to identify genes driving adolescent cortical development. The
hypothesis is that such genes are (i) enriched for GWAS signal from disorders
with adolescent onset — schizophrenia and major depression — and (ii) connected
to prior transcriptional results, specifically AHBA component C3 from earlier
work in this group and the leading component of an independent snRNA-seq
analysis.

The design choice that distinguishes this work from prior imaging genetics is
the phenotype. Previous studies have almost universally run GWAS on
*cross-sectional* brain measures — thickness or volume at one timepoint. Here
the phenotype is the *rate of change*: a per-subject slope of cortical thickness
on age, estimated from repeated scans. Release 7.0 is what makes this feasible;
it supplies enough subjects with three or more imaging visits that
subject-specific slopes carry usable signal (§4.3).

This matters because the genetic architecture of a developmental *rate* need not
resemble that of a static measure. If adolescent cortical thinning is under
genetic control, the relevant variants act on the trajectory, and a
cross-sectional phenotype averages over exactly the variation of interest.

---

## 2. Model specification

For each of 68 Desikan-Killiany cortical regions, thickness is modelled across
visits with a linear mixed model fitted in R (`lme4`), with random intercepts
*and* random slopes per subject:

```
value ~ age_c + sex + (age_c | subject)
```

`age_c` is age in years centred on the sample mean. The random-effect term
`(age_c | subject)` yields per-subject BLUPs for both intercept (thickness at
mean age) and slope (annual rate of change, mm/yr). The slope BLUPs are the
developmental phenotype; everything downstream is a function of them.

Three specification choices are deliberate and each is defended empirically in
§4: no global-thickness covariate, no family random effect, and a minimum of two
visits per subject. The settled configuration is
`configs/ct_70_noglobal_mv2_genetic.yaml`.

Modelling runs in R because `lme4`'s handling of correlated random effects and
its BLUP extraction are more dependable than the Python equivalents; all
downstream spatial, transcriptional and genetic analysis is Python. The two
communicate through parquet files in `out/<run_id>/`.

## 3. The framework reproduces across releases

Group-level developmental maps — the regional mean of subject slopes, 68 DK
regions — are compared between releases on the *same* specification
(`global_covariate: none`, `min_visits: 2`), so that a release difference is
not confounded with the specification change of §4.1.

| comparison | Spearman ρ | Pearson r |
|---|---|---|
| release 5.1 vs 7.0, matched specification | 0.937 | 0.975 |
| visit filter within 7.0: ≥2 vs ≥3 | 0.998 | 0.999 |
| family effect within 7.0: on vs off | **1.000** | **1.000** |

The release upgrade and the adapter rewrite did not disturb the cortical
pattern, so downstream differences are attributable to sample composition rather
than to pipeline changes. Mean cortical rate is −0.0185 mm/yr on 5.1 and −0.0189
on 7.0.

Two notes on reading this table. First, these are *group-level* maps — the
`age_c` fixed effect per region — not subject BLUPs, whose group mean is zero by
construction. Second, the family effect leaves the group map exactly unchanged
(ρ = 1.000) while destroying the genetic signal (§4.2): a specification can be
invisible at the group level and fatal at the subject level, which is why the
family-effect problem went undetected in the earlier draft.

An earlier version of this report gave the release correlation as 0.977 without
naming the coefficient. The Pearson value is 0.975 and the Spearman value is
0.937; the table above reports both, computed on matched specifications.

Mean whole-cortex thinning is −0.019 mm/yr on 7.0 under the settled
specification.

## 4. Design decisions, and what settled them

Three specification questions turned out to matter more than any analysis
choice downstream. Each was settled by a measurement, and in two cases the
measurement contradicted the earlier draft.

### 4.1 The global covariate should not be included

The 5.1 draft conditioned each regional model on the subject's mean thickness
across the cortex, intending to isolate regionally specific development. We do
not do that, but the reason is interpretability rather than heritability — and
that is a change from an earlier version of this report, which claimed a large
heritability cost. **That claim does not survive the pairing correction of §11.5
and is retracted here.**

With DZ twins separated from non-twin siblings, the two specifications are not
distinguishable by heritability at all:

| `min_visits` | h² no global *(settled)* | h² global mean as covariate | difference | 95% CI | P(diff ≤ 0) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| ≥ 2 | 0.444 | 0.534 | +0.089 | −0.282 to +0.453 | 0.32 |
| ≥ 3 | 0.351 | 0.427 | +0.077 | −0.368 to +0.512 | 0.38 |

The difference is bootstrapped over the *same* resampled pairs for both
specifications, so it is a paired comparison rather than two independent
intervals. It is not resolvable at either visit filter, and the point estimate
now favours the *global-adjusted* phenotype — the opposite sign to what the
earlier draft reported. Falconer's estimator has a sampling SD of roughly 0.15
here (§6), which is larger than any difference between these two designs; the
earlier apparent effect was an artefact of pooling siblings into the DZ class,
which depresses r(DZ) by an amount that depends on the phenotype.
Table: `docs/h2_global_covariate_paired.csv`.

Two arguments do survive, and they are the reason for the decision.

**The fitted coefficient means something different.** With `global_within` in
the model, a region's age coefficient is its thinning rate *relative* to
cortex-wide thinning, not an absolute rate. On 7.0 that coefficient is positive
in 24 of 68 regions — those regions thin more slowly than average, but a reader
who takes the sign at face value will conclude they thicken. Without the
covariate, the coefficient is the total rate and is negative in 67 of 68.

**The transcriptional association is carried by the shared component.** Against
AHBA C3, the directly-fitted coefficient gives ρ = −0.546 (p_spin = 0.0016)
without the covariate but only −0.143 (p_spin = 0.45) with it. The association
lives in the cortex-wide thinning the covariate removes.

There is a caveat here worth stating precisely, because it narrows the claim.
The association depends on which *coefficient* is interpreted, not only on which
model is fitted: `maps.total_age_slope()` reconstructs the absolute rate from a
global-adjusted fit as β_age + β_global · dglobal/dage, and that reconstruction
recovers ρ = −0.481 (p_spin = 0.006) from the *same* global-adjusted run. So the
covariate does not destroy the information — it moves it out of the coefficient
one would naturally read. Dropping the covariate makes the quantity of interest
the thing the model actually estimates. Table: `docs/ahba_by_global_spec.csv`.

| run | coefficient | C1 | C2 | C3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| no global *(settled)* | as fitted | −0.089 | −0.330 | **−0.546** |
| global mean as covariate | as fitted (`slope_adjusted`) | +0.126 | −0.063 | −0.143 |
| global mean as covariate | reconstructed (`slope_total`) | −0.011 | −0.316 | **−0.481** |

This decision also changes what the phenotype *is*, and hence its relationship
to everything downstream — which is the substance of the first correction in §11.

### 4.2 The family random effect destroys the genetic signal

**Any run intended for heritability or GWAS must set `family_effect: false`.**

Adding `(1 | family_id)` partitions between-family variance into its own random
effect. The subject-level BLUPs that remain are *within-family deviations* — and
the between-family component, which is exactly what genetic relatedness
explains, has been removed. With two phenotyped members per family the family
effect centres them at zero, so their deviations must sum to zero and
anti-correlate by construction.

Measured on 7.0 using the release's own genotyped pair table (266 MZ and 424 DZ
twin pairs with phenotypes; §11.5), whole-cortex thickness:

| phenotype | `(1\|family_id)` | r(MZ) | r(DZ twin) | Falconer h² |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| baseline thickness *(positive control)* | with | 0.579 | **−0.152** | **1.463** |
| baseline thickness *(positive control)* | without | 0.875 | 0.505 | 0.740 |
| developmental slope *(target)* | with | 0.473 | 0.232 | 0.482 |
| developmental slope *(target)* | without | 0.501 | 0.279 | 0.444 |

The positive control is what exposes the problem. Cortical thickness is strongly
familial, so a *negative* DZ correlation is not a weak result but a structurally
impossible one, and the h² it implies — 1.463 — is outside the parameter space.
Note the trap: on the *target* phenotype the same bug returns h² = 0.482, which
is *indistinguishable* from the correct 0.444. Had we looked only at the slope,
the artefact would have been invisible — not merely mistaken for a positive
finding, but undetectable. This is the strongest argument for keeping a positive
control in the pipeline: the bug is visible on one phenotype and silent on the
one we care about. Table: `docs/h2_family_effect_contrast.csv`.

`gcta_export.py` therefore guards on the model specification
(`FamilyEffectConflict`) rather than on the h² value, with regression tests in
`tests/test_gcta_export.py`.

Relatedness must be handled where it belongs: in the GRM for GCTA, or via a
mixed-model GWAS.

Dropping the family effect costs almost nothing in the slope estimates
themselves — BLUPs correlate r = 0.997 with and without it, at every visit
filter — so there is no tradeoff to weigh. The family effect is simply wrong
for this purpose.

![Family effect on heritability]({{artifact:art_92617adf-e522-4f53-9efd-62951ecbc602}})

### 4.3 `min_visits: 3` costs power; use ≥2

Per-subject slope reliability rises with follow-up length, but retaining fewer
subjects costs more than the reliability gain returns. Effective N — the
quantity GWAS power scales with — peaks at the permissive filter:

| run | subjects | mean visits | mean slope reliability | effective N | vs 5.1 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5.1, ≥2 visits | 8,419 | 2.31 | 0.099 | 833 | 1.00 |
| **7.0, ≥2 visits** | **8,192** | **2.86** | **0.166** | **1,361** | **1.63** |
| 7.0, ≥3 visits | 5,195 | 3.35 | 0.203 | 1,052 | 1.26 |
| 7.0, 4 visits | 1,830 | 4.00 | 0.256 | 469 | 0.56 |

Effective N = subjects × mean reliability. All four rows are matched on
specification (no global covariate, no family effect), differing only in release
and visit filter — see correction 6 in §11, which reports what an unmatched
comparison did here. 7.0 at ≥2 visits gives a 63% gain over 5.1; the ≥3-visit
filter still beats 5.1 (1.26×) but throws away 23% of the available power
relative to ≥2. 7.0's advantage is longer follow-up (2.86 versus 2.31 mean
visits) and better per-slope reliability rather than more subjects — the subject
counts are almost identical.

Crossing the visit filter with the family effect over all eight runs confirms
that the two decisions are independent — the reliability ordering is identical
with and without the family effect, and effective N is maximised at ≥2 visits
in both cases.

![Reliability design grid]({{artifact:art_949ca80d-6a2c-4113-855c-6ae97c1d489a}})

![Release power tradeoff]({{artifact:art_0dd79d35-d9cc-47a4-94a9-b8e244c49274}})

Reliability is modest in absolute terms — mean 0.166 across regions, ranging
0.033 to 0.273. This is the fundamental constraint on the whole enterprise and
§12 returns to it.

## 5. The developmental phenotype: regional maps

Under the settled specification, all 68 regions thin over adolescence — there is
no region of net thickening. Mean rate is −0.019 mm/yr; the range across regions
is −0.031 (left frontal pole) to −0.002 mm/yr (left entorhinal).

By lobe, thinning is fastest in parietal cortex (−0.022 mm/yr) and frontal
(−0.021), slowest in temporal (−0.015) and occipital (−0.017).

Between-subject variability in rate (τ, the SD of subject slopes) tracks the mean
rate closely and peaks in the same frontal-pole region (0.011 mm/yr) where it is
lowest in lingual cortex (0.004).

![Developmental maps]({{artifact:art_73b07822-69cb-441d-a80b-e8629a646bd8}})

The two rows answer different questions. Row 1 is what the phenotype *is* —
where cortex starts (baseline thickness, the model intercept at the centring
age), how fast it thins, and how much subjects differ in that rate. Row 2 is
what can be done with it — whether a per-subject slope is measurable at all
(reliability), whether it is heritable, and how it is spatially structured
(PC1). Baseline thickness autoscales from 1.8 mm rather than from 0, because a
zero-anchored scale spends its whole range on values that cannot occur and
renders the panel flat; the other sequential maps do start at 0, where 0 is the
meaningful floor.

### Components of the slope map

Principal components of the subject × region slope matrix give three
interpretable axes. Variance explained is 24.1%, 5.5% and 4.5% (see §8 for an
important note on the variance convention).

- **PC1** contrasts parietal and superior-frontal cortex (loading most
  negatively: precuneus −1.46, inferior parietal −1.36) against cingulate and
  insula (caudal anterior cingulate +1.82, entorhinal +1.86). Lobe means run
  from parietal −0.97 to insula +1.40.
- **PC2** and **PC3** carry less variance but, as §7 shows, far stronger
  transcriptional coupling.

Regional loadings are in `docs/regional_slope_pc_loadings.csv`; the full map
table with every column used below is `docs/developmental_maps_noglobal.csv`.

## 6. Heritability

All heritability here is **Falconer's estimator** — twice the difference between
MZ and DZ correlations — computed on the pair table 7.0 ships itself
(`y/gn_y_genrel.tsv`), which gives 266 MZ and 424 DZ *twin* pairs with
phenotypes. **DZ twins are not pooled with non-twin full siblings**, a change
from every earlier version of these numbers; §11.5 gives the measurement that
forced it. §12 states the residual bias; GCTA-GRM on the cluster is the proper
replacement.

Every number in this section is regenerated by
`tools/regen_h2_tables.py`, so it can be re-run rather than trusted.

**The developmental slope phenotype is heritable: h² = 0.444** (95% CI
0.181–0.717) for whole-cortex mean slope.

The positive control passes: baseline thickness gives h² = 0.740 (CI
0.575–0.916), inside the parameter space and consistent with the published twin
literature on cortical thickness. That control is what distinguishes the settled
specification from the family-effect run, where the same control returned an
impossible 1.463 (§4.2).

Candidate phenotypes:

| phenotype | h² (full sample) | 95% CI |
|---|---|---|
| baseline thickness *(control)* | 0.740 | 0.575 – 0.916 |
| global mean slope | 0.444 | 0.181 – 0.717 |
| slope PC1 | 0.441 | 0.174 – 0.699 |
| slope PC3 | 0.411 | 0.144 – 0.680 |
| slope PC2 | 0.272 | 0.001 – 0.545 |

The four slope phenotypes span 0.27 to 0.44 with intervals about 0.5 wide, so
they are **not distinguishable from each other** — the estimator's sampling SD
here is roughly 0.15. Nothing in this table selects a GWAS phenotype; §9 ranks
them on other grounds for exactly that reason.

![Heritability]({{artifact:art_d03d2023-cf1b-4e61-be6e-f683761ff1ff}})

### Per-region heritability, and why not to trust it region by region

Regional h² has median 0.266 (IQR 0.169–0.405), peaks at 0.664 in left
precentral gyrus, and is negative — an out-of-range estimate, i.e. r(MZ) < r(DZ)
— in 3 of 68 regions. By lobe it is highest parietal (0.353) and frontal (0.289),
lowest cingulate (0.137) and insula (0.209).

**The regional map is not stable enough to rank individual regions.** Split-half
Spearman correlation of the per-region h² map is 0.32 (range 0.10–0.46) across 20
family-level splits. Any claim of the form "region X is the most heritable" would
not survive resampling, and §10 shows what happens when a phenotype is built on
such a claim.

Two negative results constrain everything downstream:

1. Per-region h² is **uncorrelated with regional thinning rate** (ρ = 0.038).
   Where the cortex thins fastest is not where thinning is most heritable.
2. Per-region h² shows **no association with any AHBA component** that survives
   the spin test (§7).

Heritability and transcriptional relevance are therefore separate axes. No single
regional criterion optimises both, which is precisely why the phenotype decision
in §10 required an explicit tradeoff rather than a maximisation.

## 7. Transcriptional comparison: AHBA components

Maps are compared to the three AHBA developmental components (C1–C3) from prior
work in this group, using spatial permutation ("spin") tests with 5,000
rotations. The lowest attainable p-value is therefore 1/5001 ≈ 0.0002, and
p-values at that floor are reported as bounds, not as zero.

The comparison runs in the DK parcellation on the 34 bilateral regions where the
AHBA components are defined. This coarsens the transcriptional side
substantially — effect sizes should be read as a lower bound, and the spin test
has few points to work with.

### The replication holds

**Absolute thinning rate versus AHBA C3: ρ = −0.546, p_spin = 0.0016.** This
closely matches the thesis 5.1 result (−0.550, p = 0.004). The phenotype refit
without the global covariate recovers the original association; the sign, the
magnitude and the significance all reproduce across a release change and a full
pipeline rewrite.

### The strongest associations in the project are on the components

| map | component | ρ | p_spin |
|---|---|---|---|
| **slope PC3** | **C2** | **+0.854** | **< 0.0002** |
| **slope PC2** | **C1** | **−0.812** | **< 0.0002** |
| between-subject SD of rate | C1 | −0.505 | 0.027 |
| slope PC1 | C1 | −0.511 | 0.035 |
| absolute thinning rate | C3 | −0.546 | 0.0016 |
| per-region h² | *(all)* | — | n.s. |

Slope PC2 and PC3 track AHBA C1 and C2 at effect sizes substantially exceeding
the mean-rate/C3 replication. These are the strongest spatial associations
anywhere in this analysis, and they reordered the phenotype priority in §10:
these components carry the transcriptional signal the project is built around,
even though they are not the most heritable candidates.

All 18 map × component pairs, with both spin and naive p-values, are in
`docs/ahba_vs_maps_noglobal.csv`.

### The maps, side by side

![ABCD developmental maps and AHBA components]({{artifact:art_8dbed094-9be5-4285-926e-961c9c1e92ed}})

Each row pairs one ABCD map (left) with the AHBA component it matches most
strongly (centre) and the scatter behind that ρ (right). The scatter is what
makes the coefficient auditable: a ρ printed in a title cannot show whether it
rests on a few extreme regions or on a consistent gradient, and the surface maps
cannot either. Both axes are z-scored within each map, because the units are
incommensurable (mm/yr, an SD, unitless PC loadings, and an expression
component); z-scoring is monotone, so Spearman ρ is unchanged. The scatter uses
the 34 **bilateral** regions, matching how ρ is computed — plotting all 68
unilateral regions would show a tighter cloud than the coefficient beside it
describes.

Two things are visible here that the table alone does not convey. Slope PC3 and
AHBA C2 share a frontal-positive, occipital-negative gradient legible by eye in
the two surface panels, and the scatter shows that pair's ρ = +0.85 resting on a
consistent monotone relation across the whole range rather than on outliers. The
mean-rate/C3 association (top row) is the opposite case: it survives the spin
test, but the scatter shows it carried substantially by a handful of
high-thinning regions beyond +2 z, so it rests on a less uniform spatial
correspondence than the PC associations do. Regional h² (bottom row) is the
clearest negative — a visibly unstructured cloud, ρ = +0.31, p_spin = 0.12.

## 8. Structural covariance of developmental slopes

The 68 × 68 correlation matrix of subject slopes across regions — structural
covariance of *development*, rather than of static thickness.

The matrix is almost entirely positive: only 13 of 2,278 region pairs are
negative, and the most negative is −0.076. Mean off-diagonal r is 0.205 (median
0.186, max 0.757). Subjects who thin faster in one region thin faster nearly
everywhere, which is the same fact that makes the global covariate so damaging
(§4.1).

Coupling decomposes by anatomy:

| pair type | mean r |
|---|---|
| homotopic (same region, opposite hemisphere) | 0.455 |
| within-lobe | 0.344 |
| within-hemisphere | 0.226 |
| between-lobe | 0.182 |

Region identity dominates hemisphere: the homotopic mean (0.455) far exceeds the
within-hemisphere mean (0.226). Within-lobe coupling is strongest in parietal
(0.472) and occipital (0.458), weakest in cingulate (0.171).

![Structural covariance]({{artifact:art_0d2955df-b5cb-4c75-a383-dd322983bfe8}})

### The SC-PCs and the slope PCs are the same decomposition

Eigendecomposing the structural covariance matrix and running PCA on the
subject × region slope matrix return **identical** components: loading
correlation r = 1.0 with maximum absolute difference 0.0 for PC1–PC3, and
subject scores that match exactly. This is expected — PCA on standardised
columns *is* the eigendecomposition of their correlation matrix — but the two
are implemented by independent code paths, so the identity is a useful
cross-check. It is a coding verification, not independent evidence: the two
routes are not separate findings and must not be reported as such.

`test_sc_pcs_equal_slope_pcs` pins this to exact equality rather than a
correlation threshold, so that a refactor dropping the per-region
standardisation would fail rather than silently produce two plausible but
different maps.

### Variance convention — read this before quoting a percentage

`covariance.py` exposes two conventions and they differ by more than threefold:

| component | λ / Σλ *(standard)* | λ² / Σλ² *(as reported in the thesis)* |
|---|---|---|
| PC1 | **24.1%** | 79.8% |
| PC2 | 5.5% | 4.1% |
| PC3 | 4.5% | 2.8% |

The thesis figure of roughly 80% of variance in PC1 is the **squared**
convention. The standard convention gives 24.1%. Any statement about variance
explained must name which is meant; this report uses the standard convention
throughout except where explicitly stated.

## 9. Site and scanner effects

**What can and cannot be tested here.** Site and scanner are candidate
confounds for a longitudinal phenotype, but the two releases available locally
constrain how finely scanner can be modelled. Neither the 7.0 nor the 5.1 tree
ships a device-serial column — `mri_info_deviceserial` is absent from both,
verified by scanning the acquisition-info and motion-QC headers directly rather
than trusting a docstring. What 7.0 exposes is **manufacturer**: 4 levels
across all visits, 3 at baseline. Earlier versions of this section reported
serial-level effects (29 devices, 26.5% of subjects switching); those figures
are not reproducible and are withdrawn. Manufacturer is a coarser grouping, so
it necessarily accounts for less variance — this section is a *weaker* test
than the one it replaces, not an equivalent one. Recovering serial-level
control needs the imaging-QC table from **DAIRC** (the Data Analysis,
Informatics and Resource Center, the ABCD centre that performs the centralised
image processing and QC and distributes the derived imaging measures), which is
not in the local extract.

With that caveat, both effects are present but small. **ICC** (intraclass
correlation coefficient) is the fraction of total phenotype variance lying
*between* groups — between sites, or between scanner manufacturers — so an ICC of
0.02 means 2% of the variance in the slope phenotype is attributable to which
site a subject attended, and 98% to differences between subjects within sites.

Between-site ICC for the
global slope phenotype is **0.021** across 18 sites; baseline manufacturer gives
**0.0013** across 3 levels. Both are significant given n = 8,192 and together
account for about 2% of variance. Per region, no ICC exceeds 0.08 for site or
0.07 for manufacturer.

Manufacturer changes are uncommon: **4.2%** of subjects (346 of 8,192) change
manufacturer across follow-up in 7.0. Their global slopes are somewhat more
variable than non-switchers' (variance ratio 1.17, Cohen *d* = 0.23), but they
are too few to matter for the sample as a whole.

Critically, heritability is insensitive to what can be tested:

| phenotype variant | h² | 95% CI |
|---|---|---|
| global slope, raw *(settled)* | 0.444 | 0.182 – 0.717 |
| site-mean removed | 0.463 | 0.197 – 0.737 |
| baseline-manufacturer-mean removed (k = 3) | 0.438 | 0.186 – 0.682 |

Removing site or manufacturer means moves h² by less than 0.02, an order of
magnitude inside the confidence interval. **No site or scanner correction is
applied to the primary phenotype**, and the GWAS should not need scanner
covariates beyond the standard set — though including them is harmless.
Table: `docs/h2_site_scanner_sensitivity.csv`.

![Site and scanner effects]({{artifact:art_371877ab-0f2b-4ddd-9a39-fc2706f9a1ff}})

Top-left is the per-region site ICC on the cortical surface; the other three
panels are the two ICCs against each other, their per-region distributions, and
the slope-variance inflation in manufacturer switchers. The map and the boxplot
show the same quantity two ways, which is how to check that a small overall ICC
is not hiding a few high regions — it is not: the highest single region is 0.076.

## 10. Phenotype priority for GWAS

Which phenotype should go to GWAS first? This was re-derived from scratch on the
settled specification; the earlier ordering was computed on the global-adjusted
design and does not transfer.

**Method.** Any phenotype whose *definition* consumes the twin data must be
scored out of sample, or its heritability is inflated by selection. Across 40
splits, PCA loadings are fitted on the training half and *projected* onto the
held-out half, so no phenotype definition ever sees the twins it is evaluated on.
A "top-10 most heritable regions" phenotype is included precisely because it
violates this in the obvious way.

**Splits are taken at the pair level, keeping families whole.** Splitting by
subject would put some twin pairs' two members on opposite sides, which both
leaks the very covariance Falconer measures and drops those pairs from *both*
half-samples, silently shrinking n. This is a change from the earlier version of
this section, and it matters: see below.

| phenotype | held-out h² (SD) | vs global mean | best AHBA | definition |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| global mean slope | 0.464 (0.10) | — | C3, −0.546 | deterministic |
| slope PC1 | 0.462 (0.18) | −0.002, p = 0.95 | C1, −0.511 | deterministic |
| slope PC3 | 0.406 (0.18) | −0.058, p = 0.052 | C2, **+0.854** | deterministic |
| top-10 h² regions | 0.403 (0.13) | −0.061, p = 0.002 | none | **unstable** |
| slope PC2 | 0.263 (0.18) | −0.201, p < 0.001 | C1, **−0.812** | deterministic |

Paired t-tests over the 40 shared splits;
tables `docs/heldout_h2_by_split.csv` and `docs/heldout_h2_paired_tests.csv`.

### Why the most heritable phenotype is rejected

This is the third time this candidate has been assessed, and the reason for
rejecting it has changed twice. It is worth being explicit about that, because
the *conclusion* has been stable while the *evidence* has not.

An earlier version of this report rejected it because it collapsed out of sample
on the global-adjusted design (0.363). A later version reported that it did not
collapse on the settled design — it ranked *first* at 0.649 — and rejected it on
definitional instability instead. Both of those held-out numbers were computed
with pooled DZ pairs and subject-level splits.

Under pair-level splits with DZ twins only it ranks *fourth* at 0.403, and the
paired comparison against the global mean slope reverses sign: −0.061,
p = 0.002. Two changes contribute, and they can be separated by re-running the
pooled estimator on identical splits, where the candidate merely ties
(+0.002, p = 0.90). So its apparent advantage survives neither the pairing
correction nor pair-level splitting; roughly half the earlier gap was selection
optimism that pair-level splitting removes, and the rest was pooling.

The definitional objection is unchanged and remains the decisive one. The
optimism gap is 0.29 (in-sample 0.697 versus held-out 0.403), and only
**5.0 of 10** selected regions are re-selected across splits. The phenotype is
heritable, but its *definition* is not reproducible. For a GWAS that must be
pre-registered and replicated in an independent cohort, a phenotype whose
constituent regions change with the sample is not usable, however well it scores.

### Why transcriptional signal decides the ranking

Held-out h² spans 0.263 to 0.464 across the five candidates, with per-split SDs
of 0.10 to 0.18 — comparable to the spread itself. The top two are separated by
0.002 (p = 0.95): statistically indistinguishable across 40 paired splits. AHBA
effect sizes, by contrast, span 0.51 to 0.85 with spin p ≤ 0.035.

Heritability is nearly flat across the choice set; transcriptional coupling is
not. Since the scientific question is about *genes*, the criterion that
discriminates should drive the ranking.

### Recommended order

1. **Global mean slope.** Highest held-out h² of the five, replicates the thesis
   C3 association, zero selection risk, and needs no PCA — so it transfers to a
   replication cohort without carrying loadings across. The safe primary.
2. **Slope PC3** (C2, ρ = +0.854) and 3. **slope PC2** (C1, ρ = −0.812). By far
   the strongest transcriptional coupling in the analysis. They cost 0.06 and
   0.20 in held-out h² respectively, which GWAS power can absorb more easily
   than it can absorb a weak brain–transcriptome link. PC2 is the more
   expensive bet and should follow PC3.
4. **Slope PC1.** Held-out h² indistinguishable from the global mean (−0.002,
   p = 0.95) but weaker AHBA association, so it adds little the mean does not
   already provide.

**Top-h² regions: not recommended at any position.**

## 11. Corrections to the previous report

Several conclusions in previous versions of this document were wrong. They are
recorded here rather than quietly edited, because the pattern is instructive: in
every case the error was caught by a control or a cross-check, never by the
headline number looking wrong.

**Correction 1: the maps do resemble the AHBA components.** The previous report
concluded that developmental maps showed no transcriptional association,
contradicting the thesis. That null was computed on the global-adjusted
phenotype. Refitting without the global covariate recovers the thesis result
almost exactly (ρ = −0.546 versus −0.550) and additionally reveals two much
stronger associations on the slope components (§7). The reported null was an
artefact of the specification, not a failure to replicate.

**Correction 2: the headline heritability has been revised twice — 0.39, then
0.584, now 0.444.** The first figure came from the global-adjusted run *with* the
family effect enabled, which returns an impossible 1.508 on the positive control
(§4.2). The second corrected the design but pooled DZ twins with non-twin
siblings; §11.5 corrects that. The settled value is h² = 0.444 (CI 0.181–0.717),
and its positive control passes at 0.740.

**Correction 3: a reported mean-visit count was computed by the wrong
weighting.** `docs/fit_summary.csv` and `docs/reliability_grid.csv` reported
mean visits averaged over observation rows rather than over subjects, giving
3.06 where the subject-level mean is 2.86. Both tables have been corrected.
Effective N never used that column and is unaffected.

**Correction 4: the cross-release map correlation was unsourced.** The previous
report gave ρ = 0.977 without naming the coefficient, and no run on disk could
reproduce it — no 5.1 run had been fitted under the no-global specification. That
run has now been fitted, and the matched comparison gives Spearman ρ = 0.937 /
Pearson r = 0.975 (§3, `docs/release_map_reproducibility.csv`). The old figure
appears to have been a Pearson correlation reported as if it were the rank
correlation used elsewhere in the document.

**Correction 6: boundary fits reported spurious near-perfect reliability, and
the cross-release comparison was unmatched.** Two defects compounded in §4.3.

First, `slope_reliability` guarded against a singular fit giving
`prior_var == 0` (division by zero), but not against the more common case where
a boundary fit gives a *finite* and meaningless value. When lme4 puts the
intercept–slope correlation on the boundary (|r| → 1), τ² collapses to a
near-zero value that the conditional SD tracks almost exactly, so
1 − v/τ² → 1. In the 5.1 run this affected 11 of 68 regions, which reported
mean reliability 0.87 against 0.099 in the well-identified regions — enough to
more than double the run's mean. τ² is not identified in such a fit, so
reliability is now NaN rather than high, and two tests in
`tests/test_phenotype.py` pin the distinction between a boundary correlation and
a merely strong one (r = −0.99 is kept).

Second, the 5.1 row was taken from a *global-adjusted* run while the 7.0 rows
were no-global. Matched runs are now used throughout, and
`handoff_release_comparison` refuses to emit a row whose design does not match
rather than reporting a mismatched one; `configs/ct_51_noglobal_mv2_matched.yaml`
exists for this purpose.

Together these reverse the section's secondary conclusion. The old table showed
≥3 visits on 7.0 losing to 5.1 (0.93×); matched and unbiased, it wins (1.26×),
and 7.0's overall gain rises from 1.28× to 1.63×. The primary conclusion — use
≥2 visits — is unchanged and is now more strongly supported. **No 7.0 number
elsewhere in this report changes**: the settled run has zero boundary regions,
which is why the defect surfaced only on cross-release comparison.

### 11.5 Correction 5: DZ twins were pooled with non-twin siblings

This is the largest correction in the document, it changes every heritability
number, and it reverses one conclusion (§4.1) and one ranking (§10).

**The factual error.** `heritability.py` asserted in its own docstring that
release 7.0 ships no pairwise relatedness measure, and therefore borrowed
zygosity from the 5.1 genotype file. That is false: 7.0 ships
`y/gn_y_genrel.tsv`, with pi-hat *and* an explicit pair list. No codebook
accompanies it, so the zygosity codes were derived from the data — pi-hat
separates code 1 (≈ 1.0) from codes 2 and 3 (≈ 0.5), and the birth identifier
separates 2 from 3 perfectly and in opposite directions. Hence 1 = MZ,
2 = DZ twin, 3 = non-twin full sibling. A test in `tests/test_io.py` asserts that
*evidence* rather than the constant, so a future release renumbering the codes
fails loudly instead of silently reclassifying every pair.

**What was actually wrong.** Cross-tabulating the borrowed 5.1 labels against
7.0's own shows near-perfect agreement, so this was not a zygosity-labelling
error. The bug was in pair *construction*: the old code mapped subjects to
families and treated any family with exactly two phenotyped members as a pair.
That cannot represent a family containing a twin pair plus a third sibling, and
it silently pools DZ twins with non-twin siblings.

**Why pooling is not harmless.** Nominal additive sharing is 0.5 for both
classes, so pooling looks defensible on genetic grounds. It is not, empirically:

| phenotype | r(MZ) | r(DZ twin) | r(full sib) | Δ | 95% CI | h² twins-only | h² pooled |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| global mean slope | 0.501 | 0.279 | 0.090 | **+0.189** | +0.055 to +0.319 | **0.444** | 0.644 |
| baseline thickness *(control)* | 0.875 | 0.505 | 0.452 | +0.053 | −0.055 to +0.162 | 0.740 | 0.793 |
| slope PC1 | 0.412 | 0.192 | 0.090 | +0.101 | −0.048 to +0.245 | 0.441 | 0.564 |
| slope PC2 | 0.385 | 0.249 | 0.148 | +0.101 | −0.041 to +0.242 | 0.272 | 0.387 |
| slope PC3 | 0.430 | 0.225 | 0.174 | +0.051 | −0.088 to +0.188 | 0.411 | 0.474 |

Δ is a paired bootstrap on the *difference* of the two correlations, which is the
quantity that decides the question. DZ twins correlate more than non-twin
siblings for all five phenotypes, though the difference is resolvable only for
the global slope; the consistent sign across five non-independent phenotypes is
weak supporting evidence, not a second test. The plausible mechanism is shared
gestation and a shared prenatal environment that non-twin siblings do not have —
which makes this a real confound rather than a labelling artefact.

Because Falconer subtracts r(DZ), depressing it inflates h². The effect is
largest exactly where it matters most: **+0.20 on the primary phenotype**, versus
+0.05 on the baseline-thickness control. A pipeline validated only on the control
would not have caught this.

**What changed.** `heritability.falconer` now takes `dz_class`, defaulting to
`twins_only`, and reports `r_sib_excluded` so the choice is auditable from the
output alone. `pooled` is retained only to reproduce superseded numbers. Where
the source can supply only a pooled class — 5.1, whose pi-hat cannot distinguish
twins from siblings — the argument is ignored rather than pretending to a
distinction. All published tables are regenerated by
`tools/regen_h2_tables.py`.

**Consequences elsewhere in this document.** §4.1's heritability argument for
dropping the global covariate is retracted (the difference is now unresolvable in
*either* direction). §10's ranking changes: the top-h² composite falls from first
to fourth. §4.2's family-effect artefact survives unchanged, but its lesson
sharpens — under the corrected pairing the artefact is *invisible* on the target
phenotype (0.482 versus 0.444) while still impossible on the control.

### 11.7 Correction 7: two scanner tables outlived the analysis that made them

`site_scanner_icc_by_region.csv` and `scanner_switching_summary.csv` were the
only tables in `docs/` with no generator, and both carried serial-level
quantities — 31 serials, 26.5% of subjects switching — from the serial-level
analysis §9 withdraws as unreproducible. They were residue: the analysis was
retracted, the tables were not. So §9 asserted serial counts as findings in one
paragraph and withdrew exactly those figures two paragraphs later, and a figure
drawn from them reported a per-region scanner ICC an order of magnitude above
the global value it was supposed to summarise.

Both are now regenerated at manufacturer level — the only scanner grouping
either release ships — and the column renamed from `scanner_icc` to
`manufacturer_icc` so it cannot be misread. **What changes:** per-region scanner
ICC falls from a mean of 0.023 to 0.008, and manufacturer switching from 26.5%
to 4.2% of subjects. Both changes are in the expected direction for a coarser
grouping. §9's conclusion is unchanged — site and scanner remain small — but the
test behind it is now weaker and says so.

Every figure is now generated by `tools/regen_report_figures.py` or
`tools/regen_brain_maps.py` from a committed table, and `test_docs_provenance.py`
fails if a figure on disk has no generator, if a cited table or figure is
missing, or if §9 states a serial-level count as fact.

### The general lesson

All seven defects were caught by *controls and cross-checks*, not by inspecting
the headline numbers, which looked plausible throughout. The positive control
caught the family effect; the release comparison caught the visit weighting and
the boundary-fit reliability; the thesis replication caught the global covariate;
reading the release's own data dictionary — rather than trusting a docstring —
caught the pairing; and checking each figure's title against its own table
caught the orphaned scanner tables.

Three patterns are worth generalising. First, the pairing error was a claim
written down in code, carried forward for months, and never re-checked against
the data it described. Second, corrections 3 and 6 were both caught by the
*same* cross-release comparison, and neither was visible within 7.0 alone:
the settled run has no boundary regions, so nothing about it looked wrong. A
comparison that spans two datasets tests the code in a way that any number of
checks on one dataset cannot, which is an argument for keeping the 5.1
comparison in the pipeline permanently rather than treating it as a one-off
migration check.

Third — and this is the cheapest of the three to act on — correction 7 was
findable by an *invariant*, not by an analysis: a table with no generator is a
number nobody can re-derive, and it was the only such table in the directory.
Retracting an analysis has to include retracting its outputs, or the outputs
quietly become evidence again. The provenance tests now enforce that
mechanically, which is the appropriate place for a rule that is about
bookkeeping rather than about the brain.

## 12. Limitations

**The twin estimator remains biased, and its direction is now unclear.** The
dilution that inflated these estimates is fixed (§11.5) — the DZ class is 424
genotyped DZ twin pairs, with non-twin siblings excluded. What remains is the
equal-environments assumption, and §11.5 provides direct evidence against it:
DZ twins correlate 0.19 more than non-twin full siblings at identical nominal
sharing, which is most plausibly shared gestation. Falconer reads any such
twin-specific environment as genetic, so h² = 0.444 is still an upper bound —
but the residual bias is no longer something this design can bound. **GCTA on the
GRM is the proper replacement and is the first cluster job** (§13).

**The estimator is also imprecise.** The 95% CI on the primary phenotype spans
0.181–0.717, a width of 0.54, and the four slope phenotypes lie within one
sampling SD of each other (§6). No comparison between two designs or two
phenotypes in this report is resolvable by heritability alone; §4.1 is the
cautionary case, where an apparently large difference proved to be noise.

**Slope reliability is low.** Mean per-subject slope reliability is 0.166. This
is inherent to estimating an individual trajectory from two to four points, and
it attenuates every correlation reported here — the AHBA associations included.
It also caps GWAS power: effective N is roughly 1,361, not 8,192.

**The transcriptional comparison is coarse.** AHBA components are compared on 34
bilateral DK regions. This avoids resampling the imaging data but coarsens the
transcriptional side considerably, so effect sizes are a lower bound and the spin
test has few points. Reported ρ values are conservative.

**Per-region heritability is not interpretable region by region.** Split-half
ρ = 0.32 (§6). Use it as a map, never as a ranking.

**Structural covariance is dominated by a single global factor.** With mean
off-diagonal r = 0.205 and only 13 of 2,278 pairs negative, the matrix is close
to a one-factor structure. PC2 and PC3 explain 5.5% and 4.5% of variance — small
in absolute terms, even though they carry the strongest transcriptional
associations.

**No fMRI or multi-metric integration yet.** The framework accepts other
metrics (see `configs/t1t2_baseline.yaml`), but everything here is cortical
thickness.

## 13. What runs next, on the cluster

1. **GCTA GRM heritability** on the four recommended phenotypes, to replace the
   Falconer estimates with unbiased SNP heritability. `gcta_export.py` writes
   the phenotype and covariate files; it refuses to export from a run with the
   family effect enabled.
2. **GWAS** on the global mean slope first, then slope PC3 and PC2, using a
   mixed model to handle relatedness rather than pruning relatives.
3. **MAGMA gene-level enrichment**, then test for enrichment against SCZ and MDD
   GWAS, which is the primary hypothesis. `magma_export.py` prepares inputs.
4. **Compare the resulting gene sets** to AHBA C1–C3 and to the snRNA-seq
   leading component.
5. **Test association with SCZ and MDD directly**, by two methods whose power
   differs sharply in this sample (`hpc/06_prs.sbatch`, `hpc/05_ldsc_rg.sbatch`).

The h²-versus-transcription tradeoff in §10 means step 3 should be run on both
the global mean *and* the components — they may implicate different genes, and
which one connects to the disorder GWAS is the empirical question the project
exists to answer.

### Which disorder-association test to believe

Polygenic score association is the **primary** test and genetic correlation the
secondary one, for a reason that follows directly from the reliability numbers
in §3 and §10. LDSC's rg needs both traits well-estimated, and the slope side is
not: at N≈8,000 with slope reliability ≈0.21 the expected h² z-score is ≈2,
below the z > 4 that LDSC's own guidance requires. A rg of 0.1 ± 0.4 would not
distinguish absence of overlap from absence of information.

A polygenic score moves the statistical burden to the discovery GWAS
(SCZ ≈160k, MDD ≈674k), so the score enters as one well-estimated regressor and
only the target-side regression spends our N. That makes a null informative.
`baseline_thickness` (reliability ≈0.90) is the exception where rg *is*
interpretable, and it therefore serves as the positive control for the LDSC step;
the pipeline's summary flags every row `underpowered` from its own h² z-score so
this distinction cannot be lost when reading the table.

Because the SCZ/MDD discovery GWAS are predominantly European and scores
transfer poorly across ancestry, the EUR subset is primary and the full
multi-ancestry sample is reported as a sensitivity analysis — ancestry-PC
adjustment controls stratification but does not repair transferability. See
`hpc/README.md` for the full rationale and the clumping/threshold choices.
